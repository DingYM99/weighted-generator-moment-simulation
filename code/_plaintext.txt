abstract
Saturation and feedback enter stochastic models as drift terms with
polynomial denominators, as in the incidence rates of epidemic models
and the Hill terms of gene-regulatory networks. Such denominators stop
the backward Kolmogorov generator from mapping monomials to
polynomials, and that is the obstacle which usually excludes rational
drift from moment-equation treatments. We attach to the monomial
observables a common polynomial weight assembled from those
denominator factors; the generator then acts polynomially on the
weighted observables, while the drift and the state variables are left
as they are, and the hierarchy that results is exact and implicit, in
that it involves only raw moments and their time derivatives.
Admissibility of a weight reduces to a sufficient condition with a
componentwise-minimal solution, degree-growth estimates bound the
orders a retained set can generate, and the closure order obeys
<MATH> . An algorithm reads the model specification
and returns the coefficient arrays together with the index sets of
moments and moment derivatives left unresolved by the retained
identities, all before a closure is chosen; imposing one closure map
on an unresolved moment and on its time derivative at the same time
converts a finite selection of those identities into a small
deterministic mass-matrix system, used below as a low-dimensional
surrogate for direct path simulation of the low-order statistics. For a stochastic susceptible--infectious model
with saturated incidence the closed five-dimensional system reproduces
a <MATH> -path Milstein reference with a mean normalized
root-mean-square error of <MATH> at
<MATH> , against <MATH> for the linear
noise approximation, while requiring about two orders of magnitude
less wall-clock time than the <MATH> -path simulation that
attains comparable accuracy. Every reported error is
accompanied by the Monte Carlo resolution floor below which
differences are not resolvable; in the weak-noise regime the errors of
the two closures tested lie inside that floor and must be regarded as
indistinguishable. A weight-degree ablation shows that the accuracy
gap tracks the closure burden rather than the algebraic form of the
representation. A three-dimensional miRNA-mediated gene-regulatory
model with two distinct Hill denominators is used as a second test.
Introduction
Saturation, finite contact capacity, cooperative activation and
inhibitory feedback are all written into stochastic models as drift
terms with polynomial denominators: saturated incidence in epidemic
models , Hill-type transcriptional
feedback in gene regulatory networks , and
Holling-type functional responses in interacting populations
. The denominators are not a cosmetic choice of
parametrisation. They set the location and stability of equilibria,
the amplification of fluctuations and the way uncertainty propagates
between state components, and they also obstruct the systematic
derivation of moment equations, which is the route most often taken
once the full density is out of reach.
The probability density carries the complete statistical information,
but its Fokker--Planck equation is rarely solvable in closed form and
is expensive to discretize in more than two or three dimensions
. In practice one frequently needs
only a handful of low-order statistics---means, variances and
covariances---which already describe the average response, the
intensity of fluctuations and the transmission of uncertainty between
components, and which serve uncertainty quantification, sensitivity
analysis and parameter studies .
Working with those expectations rather than with the density or with
sampled paths is what moment methods do
; the price is an
evolution equation for every moment one retains.
For It\^o systems with polynomial coefficients the generator maps
monomials to polynomials, so the generator identity produces an
explicit raw-moment hierarchy . The
hierarchy is infinite and still needs truncation and closure, but its
structure is transparent and can be closed by Gaussian, cumulant,
derivative-matching, variational or entropy-based schemes
. With
rational drift the generator no longer preserves polynomiality:
denominator-dependent expectations appear that are not finite linear
combinations of raw moments. Beyond the usual truncation and closure
problem there is therefore a representation problem, namely how to
write exact evolution equations in raw moments and their derivatives
alone while keeping the rational nonlinearity intact.
Relation to simulation-based approaches
It is useful to place the present construction among the other
deterministic surrogate routes and the sampling routes available for the same
task, because they differ in what they compute, in their cost, and in
where they fail.
Direct path simulation. Stochastic simulation algorithms for
discrete reaction networks and
numerical integration of the associated It\^o or Langevin equations
estimate moments by averaging over
sample paths. The approach is assumption-free and applies to any
drift, but the sampling error decays only as <MATH> , so
resolving a relative error of <MATH> in a second moment typically
requires <MATH> -- <MATH> paths and a time step small enough that
the discretization bias is itself below the target. This is the cost
against which the closed moment system is compared in
Section .
Generalized polynomial chaos and stochastic Galerkin.
Spectral methods expand the solution in polynomial chaos bases and
solve a coupled deterministic system for the expansion coefficients
. They are most effective when the
randomness enters through a moderate number of parameters and
the solution depends smoothly on them; the size of the coupled system
grows combinatorially with the stochastic dimension, and the
expansion targets the parameter-to-solution map rather than the
intrinsic It\^o noise considered here. The present method instead
expands in the state monomials themselves and does not introduce a
parametric stochastic dimension.
Linear noise approximation. The linear noise approximation
linearizes the drift about the
deterministic trajectory and propagates a Gaussian covariance. It is
extremely cheap and is the natural baseline for a cheap surrogate, so
it is included in every comparison below. Its limitation is that
linearization discards the nonlinear correction to the mean, which is
precisely what the retained second-order moments are meant to
capture; the numerical results quantify how large that discarded
correction is.
Moment closure without polynomialization. Denominator clearing
in the chemical master equation , distribution-based
approximations for rational models , derivative
matching for polynomial and trigonometric systems
, coupled mean-and-variance propagation
, and semidefinite-programming bounds obtained
through polynomial embedding all address the same
closure problem from different sides; see for a
general review. In population and epidemic models, where the drift is
rational because of saturation, moment closure has a long parallel
history and the same
structural difficulty appears: the generator produces moments of
order higher than those retained, and the number of such moments is
not known in advance. The contribution here is upstream of closure:
it removes the denominator from the generator action itself, so that
closure is applied to a smaller and explicitly bounded set of
moments. Related moment-dynamics formulations by the present authors
for rational and non-Gaussian systems
proceed by model-specific algebra; the point of the present
construction is that the algebra is generated once for a class and
carries computable bounds.
Contribution and outline
We develop a generator-based algebraic framework for
finite-dimensional It\^o systems with rational drift and polynomial
diffusion covariance. A common denominator-eliminating weight makes
the generator action polynomial while the drift and the state
variables are left untouched, and the resulting hierarchy is exact.
Four points are worth separating from the algebraic construction
itself, because they are what determine whether the construction is
usable as a simulation tool:
enumerate
[(i)] Structural bounds. Sufficient weight conditions,
a component-wise minimal admissible weight, degree-growth
estimates and an explicit closure-order bound
<MATH> are established, so the size of the
closed system is known before it is assembled.
[(ii)] Automated assembly. An algorithm generates the
selected identities, their coefficient arrays, and the index
sets of unresolved moments and unresolved moment derivatives
from the model specification alone; the identities are exact
before closure.
[(iii)] Consistent closure of moments and moment
derivatives. The same closure map is applied to an
unresolved moment and to its time derivative, so the left- and
right-hand sides of the implicit identity are approximated in
a compatible way. Without this, the closure injects an
uncontrolled drift into the retained dynamics.
[(iv)] Use as a simulation surrogate, with cost and
resolution stated. The closed system is a small
deterministic ODE system that can be integrated at a cost
independent of the number of paths. We report the numerical
settings needed to reproduce it, the wall-clock cost against
path simulation, the conditioning of the mass matrix along the
solution, and, for every error figure, the Monte Carlo
resolution floor that determines which differences are
meaningful.
enumerate

Conclusion
Rational drift prevents the backward Kolmogorov generator from
mapping monomials to polynomials, and this is the reason moment
methods are usually said not to apply to the stochastic models that
arise in epidemiology and gene regulation. We have shown that a
single polynomial weight built from the denominator factors removes
the obstruction: the generator applied to a weighted monomial is
again a polynomial, so an exact hierarchy exists in which only raw
moments and their time derivatives appear. The drift, the diffusion
and the state variables are not modified, and no lifting to an
extended state is required. Sufficient admissibility conditions
identify a componentwise-minimal weight, degree-growth estimates
bound the orders that a retained set can generate, and the bound
<MATH> fixes the closure order before any
computation is carried out. An algorithm assembles the coefficient
arrays and the index sets of unresolved moments and unresolved moment
derivatives from the model specification alone at a symbolic cost
that we report, and applying one closure map consistently to both an
unresolved moment and its time derivative turns a finite selection of
the identities into a small deterministic mass-matrix system.
The numerical study was arranged so that the claims can be checked.
Every reference value is a Milstein path simulation reported with its
standard error, every error figure is accompanied by the Monte Carlo
resolution floor below which differences are not resolvable, all
integrator and sampling settings are stated, and the conditioning of
the mass matrix is reported along the whole trajectory together with
a sufficient nonsingularity criterion and a least-squares fallback.
On the two examples the closed systems reproduce the path-simulation
reference to within a few times the resolution floor at weak noise
and to within about one percent in relative terms at moderate noise,
and they do so at a wall-clock cost about two orders of magnitude
smaller than a path simulation of comparable accuracy. In the
weak-noise regime the two closures tested lie inside the resolution
floor and must be regarded as indistinguishable; the comparison
against the linear noise approximation is the one place where a clear
ordering survives at all noise levels tested.
The algebraic step above is exact. What is not exact, and what limits
the range of the closed system, sits outside it and comes from three
separate places. First, the closure is not derived from the model. The algebraic step is exact, and the
weight-degree ablation shows that degrading it by raising the weight
degree, which enlarges <MATH> and the number of unresolved
moments, degrades accuracy monotonically; but the error that remains
at moderate and strong noise is closure error, and nothing in the
framework controls it. Second, neither closure enforces
realizability: the Gaussian and derivative-matching maps do not
guarantee that the retained vector stays in the moment cone, and we
have observed non-physical transients on the <MATH> time
scale when the noise is large. Third, the symbolic stage grows
combinatorially with the state dimension and the retained order, so
the method as implemented is a low-order, low-dimensional technique;
the cost figures of Section are the honest statement
of where that boundary currently lies.
Practical guidance.
For a practitioner deciding whether to use the construction, the
evidence supports the following. Use the componentwise-minimal
admissible weight of
Proposition ; we found no accuracy advantage
for a non-minimal weight in the family tested, and a larger weight
raises <MATH> and degrades accuracy monotonically. Prefer the
derivative-matching closure when the distribution is expected to be
appreciably non-Gaussian or when the noise is moderate to strong; the
Gaussian closure is adequate and slightly cheaper to assemble in the
weak-noise regime, where the two are in any case indistinguishable
within the Monte Carlo resolution. Check <MATH> along the
trajectory before trusting a run; the closed system should be treated
as a surrogate for the low-order statistics and not as a substitute
for path simulation when the full distribution, rare events or
long-time tails are the objects of interest. When the reported error
is within a factor of about <MATH> of the Monte Carlo resolution
floor, no ordering between methods should be claimed.
Outlook.
Several directions follow directly from the limitations. A closure
that respects the moment cone, or a projection onto it, would remove
the non-physical transients; an error indicator built from the
closure residual could drive adaptive selection of the retained
index set instead of the fixed order used here; and the same
weighted-generator argument applies to drift terms with several
distinct denominators, as Example 2 shows, which suggests an
extension to models with state-dependent denominators that are only
locally bounded away from zero.
